Hand Evaluation

The Bergen method for starting-hand points, adjustments once a fit is confirmed, and the New Losing Trick Count used to validate the final level.

♠Bergen — Starting Points

  • HCP: A=4, K=3, Q=2, J=1
  • Length points: +1 for a 5-card suit, +2 for 6-card, +3 for 7-card, etc.
  • Suit quality: +1 for every 4+ card suit holding 3 of the top 5 honours
  • Dubious honours: −1 for K, Q, J, Qx, Jx, KQ, AJ, KJ, QJ
  • Honour position vs. the bidding: +1 with Kx or AQ in a suit that RHO has opened or overcalled; −1 with Kx or AQ in a suit that LHO has opened or overcalled (Kx here means any suit combination without a singleton K; AQ can also be AQ doubleton, AQx, AQxx, etc.)

Additional Adjustments

  • Flatness: −1 if your hand has ≥2 cards in every suit (suit contract); −1 only with 4333 shape in an NT contract
  • Honour adjustment: count (Aces+Tens) minus (Queens+Jacks). Difference >2 → +1 HCP, >5 → +2 HCP; <−2 → −1 HCP, <−5 → −2 HCP (Aces and Tens are undervalued; Queens and Jacks are overvalued)
  • Honours opposite partner shortness are wasted
  • Shortness opposite partner honours are also wasted
  • Honours (other than the Ace) opposite an opponent's long suit are also wastage
  • Length in an opponent's suit is also wasted, since you can't develop your own long suits with your partner
  • If partner takeout doubles and you have more losers in that opponent suit (implying no wasted values opposite partner's shortness), it's good for us and we can stretch bidding

♥Practice: Count Bergen Points and NLTC

Deal a random 13-card hand, work out its Bergen starting points (HCP + length + suit quality − dubious honours) and its NLTC losers, then check yourself. Declarer/dummy points aren't needed here.

♣Declarer Points (after 8+ fit confirmed)

  • +1 per doubleton, +2 per singleton, +4 per void
  • +1 for every trump above an 8-card fit
  • +1 for every 4+ card side suit

♦Dummy Points (after 8+ fit confirmed)

  • +1 for every trump above an 8-card fit
  • +1 per doubleton, +2 per singleton
  • +1 point per total trump held, for every void

Guidelines & Game/Slam Levels

  • Open only with 12+ Bergen points
  • Regardless of Bergen points, a 1-level suit opening needs 10+ HCP for sure
  • Regardless of Bergen points, a 1NT opening needs 14+ HCP for sure
  • Regardless of Bergen points, a 1-level response should have 4+ HCP for sure
  • In the 3rd seat, we can also open with 10 HCP
  • Respond in a new suit or NT with 6+ points; with a fit, respond at the 2-level with 6–9 and at the 3-level with 10–12
  • Balanced hand test: the two longest suits together add up to 8 cards or fewer
  • Use the New Losing Trick Count to validate the final level once a fit is found
  • Game in a major: 26+ Bergen points
  • Game in a minor: 29+ Bergen points
  • Game in NT: 26+ Bergen points
  • Small slam (NT or suit): 33+ Bergen points

Slam-Bidding Toolkit

  • Kickback and Gerber ask for key cards/aces once a suit or NT slam is in view; DEPO takes over if opponents interfere over either ask.
  • 5NT — Pick a Slam lets partner choose the denomination when the slam is on but the strain isn't settled.
  • Need at least 3 aces between the two hands and no 2 immediate losers before bidding a small slam in a major; a 7NT grand slam with 13 top tricks is safer than a trump-suit grand, since the opening lead can't be ruffed away.

New Losing Trick Count (NLTC)

Applicable only after a confirmed 8-card fit. Count 1.5 losers per missing Ace, 1 per missing King, 0.5 per missing Queen. Subtract one loser with a confirmed 9+ card trump fit; subtract both hands' losers from 19 to set the suit-contract level.

How the NLTC Method Works

  • Step 1 — count losers, one hand at a time. For each suit, look only at your top 3 cards: a missing Ace costs 1.5 losers, a missing King costs 1 loser, a missing Queen costs 0.5 losers. A suit with fewer than 3 cards is only judged on the cards actually held.
  • Step 2 — add both hands' losers together. This only makes sense once an 8-card (or better) trump fit is confirmed between the two hands.
  • Step 3 — adjust for extra trumps. With a confirmed 9-card (or longer) trump fit, subtract one loser from the combined total.
  • Step 4 — subtract from 19. 19 minus the combined loser count gives the recommended contract level (e.g. a combined 7.5 losers → bid to the 19 − 7.5 = 11.5, i.e. a game-level contract).
  • Why "New" NLTC? The half-point granularity (1.5 / 1 / 0.5, instead of the old flat 1-loser-per-honour count) gives a finer, more accurate level — this is what the "New NLTC" and "LTC" columns below compare.
  • Use NLTC to validate the level once a fit is found — but blend it with hand evaluation (honour location, concentrated vs. scattered honours) rather than following it blindly.

Overcalls, Doubles & Competitive Auctions

How the partnership competes once an opponent has entered the auction.

Competing at the 3-Level

  • Never bid 3-level over an opponent's 3-level bid when we are vulnerable — even with an 8-card or 9-card fit
  • Bid at the 3-level over a 2-level auction only with a 9-card fit (both vulnerable and non-vulnerable) — do not do so with only an 8-card fit

How to Identify a Takeout Double from a Penalty Double?

  • For contracts at the 3-level or below: if the double is the very first double made by you or partner in the auction, it can never be for penalty — it's always takeout
  • For contracts at the 4-level or above (and 3NT) by the opponents: all doubles are for penalty

♠Overcalls

  • Mandate: if partner opens a 1-level suit and an opponent overcalls at any level, responder with 10+ points must bid something — at minimum, 1NT with a stopper in the opponent's suit if there's no long suit to overcall with. Opener will have 12-16 points minimum, so passing with 10+ risks missing games.
  • 1-level overcall: 8–17 points, suit quality 7+ (can be a 4-carder)
  • 2-level overcall: 12–17 points, suit quality 8+
  • 1NT immediate overcall: 15–17 with a stopper
  • Jump overcalls: weak, 6–11 points
  • Overcalls can be a bit lighter (a king shy) if partner or yourself has passed earlier
  • Michaels Cue Bid — 5-5 two-suiter. 2♣ over 1♣ and 2♦ over 1♦ promise 5+ in both majors; 2♥ over 1♥ promises 5+ spades and a minor, 2♠ over 1♠ promises 5+ hearts and a minor
  • Unusual 2NT — 5-5 in the two lowest unbid suits, 9+
  • Leaping Michaels — near game force, 18+; see the dedicated table below for the exact suits shown

♥Redouble

After partner's 1-level suit opening is doubled by an opponent, responder's redouble shows 10+ points with no 8-card fit found yet — it keeps the auction alive and sets up for penalty if the opponents compete further.

♣Takeout Double

Double: 12+ points and 3+ card support for the unbid suits.

♦Double and then Overcall

  • Double then an NT overcall: 18–20 with a stopper
  • Double then a suit overcall: 18–20, unbalanced
  • Double then a jump overcall in NT: 21+ with a stopper
  • Double then a jump suit overcall: 21+, unbalanced

♣Negative Double

After partner's 1-level opening and a weak jump overcall by the opponent, our double is negative — not penalty, even at the 3-level.

  • 1-level negative double: 6+ points
  • 2-level negative double: 9+ points
  • 3-level negative double: 12+ (game force)

♦Advancer (Overcaller's Partner)

  • Cue bid of opener's suit: shows 8 losers or fewer
  • Direct support of the overcalled suit: below 8 points

Penalty Doubles

  • Any double of a 3-level or higher suit contract is for penalty — the one exception is a double after an opponent's 3-level preempt opening, which is for takeout instead.
  • We judge penalty doubles using the Rule of 11 and the Rule of 13.

Exhaustive Types of Doubles in Our System

  1. 1st or 2nd level, after an opponent's bid — takeout double
  2. 3-level, after an opponent's preempt opening — takeout, 15+
  3. 3+ level, after a normal bid — penalty
  4. Interference over our 1NT — 12+, without a stopper
  5. Opener's rebid double after interference — 15+, without a stopper
  6. Double of an artificial bid — lead-directing
  7. Opponent opens 1NT, we double — hearts and a minor
  8. After partner opens 2♣ or 2NT and opponents interfere, double at the 3-level is game force without a stopper

Defending Against Opponents' Bids

  • TONT — vs a strong/weak opposing 1NT, and vs 1♣ Precision; see the dedicated table below for the full structure. Use only with ≥8 HCP from the 2nd seat; prefer it when non-vulnerable and avoid it from the 4th seat.
  • Mathe — over an opponent's strong 2♣: 2♦ = hearts and spades, 2♥ = hearts and a minor, 2♠ = spades and a minor, 2NT = both minors, and any 3-level bid = a natural 6+ card suit
  • Rule of 2 to overcall from the balancing (4th) seat over 1NT: interfere with 6+ starting points, or with 2+ shortness points (a singleton or void) regardless of vulnerability — otherwise pass
  • Balancing seat, passout position — 2NT shows both minors, Double shows both majors, bidding your own suit shows 5+ cards
  • Stolen Bid — partner doubles when opponents interfere over our 1NT opening
  • If our 1NT opening is doubled, all our conventions (Puppet Stayman and transfers) stay ON

Defence

Opening leads, discards, and signalling priorities.

Lines of Defence

Both defenders should identify the right line of defence — from the bidding, the likely layout of the hands, and declarer's plan — before the opening lead, again once dummy is tabled, and should keep re-checking it as the hand unfolds. When in doubt, go with passive defence in general.

  • Forcing Defence
    • Best when declarer has a two-suiter, or is in a 4-3 trump fit or a 4-4 fit with a 4-1 break
    • Lead your partnership's longest/strongest suit to make declarer ruff and run short of trumps
    • Dummy should hold a weak suit to force through, and shouldn't be short in that suit — if dummy can ruff it, forcing there just helps declarer
    • Holding the trump ace, hold it up until dummy is out of trumps (often the 3rd round) rather than draw it early
  • Active Defence
    • Needed when declarer or dummy has a long side suit, or your tricks look like they could disappear
    • Used against suit slam contracts
    • Be aggressive when a side suit is breaking well or the bidding showed slam interest
    • We voluntarily break new suits ourselves to take our tricks before they vanish
    • Against a gambling 3NT, lead an ace and race for 5 quick tricks
    • If dummy has a long, strong suit, we may at times even have to overtake and lead into weakness to have any chance of making some tricks
    • We will need to lead away from honours if necessary to promote tricks
    • Knock out entries in side suits so that dummy cannot enter to cash their long suit tricks
  • Passive Defence
    • Right when you want declarer to break new suits themselves
    • Best when dummy is balanced, declarer has no strong side suit, or it's a misfit
    • Leading trumps is generally a passive defence
    • We keep returning the same suit rather than breaking new ones
    • Essential against 6NT and grand slams
    • Identify this line when the opponents have stopped in a part-score at the 3-level or below and points are roughly evenly split between both pairs
    • Avoid leading a 4-card suit with only one honour; a 5-card suit headed by an honour is a better risk
  • Cutting Down on Ruffing
    • Lead trumps when your side holds a trump stack, on misfits, or when you control everything except trumps (opponents sacrificing)
    • Also lead trumps when dummy's bidding suggests it will ruff (avoided NT, or bid two suits and raised a third), or when you control their side suit
    • Favour a trump lead against two-suited hands like Michaels or Unusual 2NT, unless the opponents clearly have a big fit
    • If declarer is not drawing trumps, we should draw them ourselves
    • If declarer's contract involves a preempt bid, or declarer has made a sacrifice over our game, lead trumps and draw them
    • If we've penalty doubled the opponents, lead trumps
  • Creating Trump Tricks
    • Look for this when you're short (singleton/doubleton) in an unbid suit, or long in their bid-and-raised side suit (partner rates to be short there)
    • Also worth trying when your hand is weak with nothing better to try
    • Look for an uppercut — forcing declarer or dummy to ruff high — to promote a trump honour in your or partner's hand
    • Don't chase ruffing tricks if you already hold natural trump tricks (e.g. QJ10)
    • A high trump entry helps you cash in on a short holding

♠Opening Leads

  • 4th highest in a notrump contract
  • 3rd/5th highest in a suit contract
  • Do not lead away from honours
  • Always play the lower of touching honours — A to T (e.g. with J-T lead the T; with A-Q-J lead the J)
  • Against a NT contract, with 3 or fewer small cards in a suit, lead the top card

♥Passive Defence

When there's no attractive attacking lead, passive defence usually produces the best long-term results. Good passive leads:

  • A trump
  • A suit headed only by small cards
  • A suit containing no unsupported honour
  • Don't lead a bare Ace from Axx

♣Discards

Give attitude first (a low card shows no interest in the discarded suit), then show count with the second card discarded in that suit (low is odd, high is even).

♦Signal Order

  1. Attitude
  2. Count, when declarer plays a suit
  3. Suit preference
Work out declarer's shape and points as early in the defence as possible.

Classic Defensive Guidelines

  • Second hand low — as second to play to a trick, play low and keep your options open rather than rising with an honour.
    • Second hand high exceptions:
    • When you need to cover an honour, provided it would surely promote an honour trick (including the Ten) for partner or us
    • When you have two touching honours, split them and play the honour in second hand instead of playing low
  • Third hand high — as third to play, play your highest card (unless it can't beat anything useful) to try to force out or promote an honour.
    • Third hand high exceptions:
    • If dummy is visible and holds, say, the Queen, play the Jack with a holding like AJx rather than the Ace — keeping an honour in reserve to cover dummy's honour
    • If dummy and partner are both short in a suit and declarer has length, while you also hold length with a holding like Kxxx or Qxxx, you can play third hand low instead — your honour will eventually be promoted. Playing third hand high here means you won't get any winner in that suit at all
  • Cover an honour with an honour — covering can promote a lower honour in your or partner's hand; don't cover if it can't possibly promote anything.
  • Lead through strength, up to weakness — lead toward dummy's weak holding or through declarer's known strong suit to set up finesses for partner.
  • Return partner's suit — normally the higher of touching honours, or your original fourth-best, so partner can read the remaining count.
  • With a doubleton left in a suit, always return or lead the higher of the two cards
  • Surrounding Play: when leading a suit into weakness in dummy, lead a card higher than dummy's highest card in that suit — this can trap an honour and promote a trick for partner. This applies to the trump suit as well
  • Win as cheaply as possible — don't spend an unnecessary high card when a lower one wins the trick just as well.
  • Don't break a new suit needlessly — when you have a safe exit, avoid opening a suit that could cost a trick; let declarer do the work.
  • When in doubt, play third hand high or return partner's suit — the two safest defaults when you don't have a clear read on the hand.

General Principles

The guiding ideas behind the whole system — from the July 2026 unlearnings review.

  • Slow and fast arrival — with strong hands, show strength first and fit second; bid slowly with strong hands, fast with weaker ones. E.g. 1♠–4♠ is a weak hand with no slam interest; 1♠–2♣–2♦–4♠ is strong.
  • Never lie about strength — when you can lie temporarily about shape instead. For example, opener's rebid of the same suit may sometimes be with only a 5-carder.
    • Example 1: Opener has 4432 shape with 12-14 points in S-H-D-C. Opener opens 1♦ and responder bids 1NT. Now opener's rebid can only be 2♣, with a doubleton. Any other rebid becomes a reverse — we shouldn't lie about strength (lying about shape is OK).
    • Example 2: Opener has 4522 shape with 12-14 points in S-H-D-C. Opener opens 1♥ and responder bids 1NT. Now opener's rebid can only be 2♣ or 2♦, with a doubleton. Any other rebid becomes a reverse — we shouldn't lie about strength (lying about shape is OK).
    • Example 3: When partner has game-forced the auction, opener can rebid in NT with an unbalanced hand if any other available bid would become a reverse.
  • If opener has to lie, prefer lying about shape, not strength — rebidding the opened suit with a 5-carder as if it were a 6-carder is a smaller error than reversing or rebidding 1NT, since they falsely claim higher strength or a balanced hand when the hand is actually unbalanced and not strong — a far more serious misdescription.
  • Responder is the temporizer, opener is the describer — responder cannot jump to show values, but opener can.
  • Once partner has passed — any subsequent bids by you or by your partner will be a King (3 points) shy of what that bid usually promises.

Alerts for Live Tourneys

  • Pre-alert before the deal — announce the system as "SAYC 2-over-1"
  • Alert the 1NT opening range, and that NT rebids can include a 4-card major or a partner fit
  • Ask the opponent of the bidder for an explanation, not the bidder
  • Only explain an alert if asked, and only in the opponent's ear
  • Never correct partner's wrong alert explanation before the auction ends
  • As declarer, clarify and explain every alert to defenders before the opening lead
  • As defenders, explain to declarer after the deal but before scores are agreed (integrity!)
  • Dummy's sole job is tracking tricks won and lost until the 13th card, before returning cards to the board
  • Don't alert slam-asking bids during the auction — offer to explain before the opening lead
  • Artificial, takeout, and penalty doubles need no alert

Teams / IMPS — General Tips

Scoring by IMPs changes the risk calculus from matchpoints: single hands matter less, and the shape of the outcome (make/down, not overtricks) matters more.

♠Bidding Philosophy

  • Bid close games — especially when vulnerable, which feels counter-intuitive. This is the platinum rule for winning at IMPs.
  • Stretch to bid a vulnerable game even at a 40% chance; a non-vulnerable game at 50%. If you have to think about it, bid game.
  • Bid thin games, but avoid thin slams — only bid slams that are at least 75%.
  • Overcalls should be sound — upgrade by roughly a Queen versus what you'd need at matchpoints.
  • Preempts can be lighter, since they mainly deny opponents room to find games and slams.
  • With an 8-card fit and non-vulnerable, be willing to compete to the 3-level.
  • Don't stretch part scores, especially when vulnerable, and don't make wild preemptive bids just because you hold a long suit.

♥Competing & Doubling

  • Overbidding costs more than underbidding at IMPs — when in doubt, prefer the safer, more normal action.
  • Don't double their partscore unless you have it beaten in your own hand (a fistful of trump tricks) — never double on high cards alone.
  • No penalty doubles unless the opponents have clearly sacrificed.
  • If opponents have already bid to game, don't sacrifice — but interfere more before they get there, especially once partner has passed.
  • Sacrifice more when you're non-vulnerable and they're vulnerable.
  • With 18 points when the opponents open, double and plan to find the best game — assume 6 points opposite even if partner passes.

♣Declarer Play

  • The moment dummy comes down, check how a bad trump break would affect the contract and plan for that contingency first.
  • Play safety lines over greedy ones — duck a trick early if it guarantees the contract, rather than risk it for an overtrick.
  • Test suits for bad breaks by leading toward your honours, and keep the hand that plays after the "danger" opponent able to finesse through.
  • Make or set the contract — don't worry about over- or under-tricks once you're in the right spot.
  • Play your safest contract: a making minor-suit partial beats a risky major-suit one — plus 110 vs plus 140 rarely decides a match.

♦Defence & Team Mentality

  • As a defender, it's worth taking a risk (like leading an unsupported King) that you wouldn't try at matchpoints, if it has a real chance to defeat the contract.
  • You have partners at the other table — don't make a risky bid or play just because you think your team is behind in the match.
  • There will be a hand each match where both sides can make game — usually a wild, distributional deal. It's usually right to bid one more, unless you're sure they're going down.
  • Don't be a hero: avoid a confusing call that might get passed when it's forcing, or that overstates your values.
  • The player holding your cards at the other table faces the same tough decision — if it's hard for you, it's hard for them too.

Matchpoints — General Tips

Matchpoint (MP) scoring rewards frequency and relative comparison on every single board — it changes the risk calculus quite differently from IMPs.

♠Bidding

  • Prefer notrump over a minor suit for the same likely trick count — 3NT beats 5♣ on shared boards (same or fewer tricks needed, and the field will mostly be in NT too)
  • Don't bid thin games/slams just because the odds are positive-EV in the IMP sense — at MP, going down in a game the field is stopping short of costs you the whole board with no partial credit; conversely, making an "automatic" game the field also bids gains you nothing, so look for the contract that's different and better, not just "correct"
  • Be more willing to compete one level higher for a making partscore — an extra 50-100 points in a partial swings matchpoints hugely, since so many pairs are flat on the same auction
  • Preempt aggressively — making the opponents guess in an uncontested-feeling auction is worth more at MP than at IMPs, since a guess wrong by them is a near-top for you even if it's not a game/slam swing

♥Play

  • Take the line that maximizes tricks, not the line that maximizes safety, whenever the contract is comfortable to make anyway — an overtrick is real money at MP; at IMPs you'd automatically take a safety play costing a trick to guarantee the contract, but at MP that same safety play is often wrong once making is close to certain either way
  • Reserve safety plays for hands where the contract itself is genuinely in doubt
  • In no-play/marginal hands, play for the layout most likely to be true rather than the layout that only saves your contract — MP rewards frequency, not worst-case protection

♦Defense

  • Double competitive partscores more readily when confident — converting their making partial into a penalty beats the field's plus score directly
  • Avoid speculative sacrifices — a "cheap" IMP save can be a flat-out bottom at MP if the field is defending profitably or making their own contract
  • Fight harder for exact-value penalties (don't let overtricks slip on defense) since the difference between +100 and +150 against you is a full swing in the matchpoint scale

Balancing — Matchpoints-Specific

Balancing is a matchpoints tool, not an IMPs one — it's worth doing here in a way it usually isn't at IMPs. Balance from the passout seat based on these guidelines:

  • If we're vulnerable, don't let opponents play a suit contract at 2♦ or below
  • If we're non-vulnerable, don't let opponents play a suit contract at 2♠ or below
  • Bid 2NT in the passout seat to show both minors
  • Bid Double to show both majors
  • Bid your own suit with 5+ cards and 7+ suit quality
All of this is subject to the opponents having found a fit. Balancing is not applicable if opener has preempted, and not applicable if the opponents have stopped at 1NT.

Doubling/Redoubling "Into" Game

  • If the contract/trick points applicable for the doubled/redoubled contract are ≥100, we're also eligible for the bonus applicable for making a game
  • Doubling into game — 2M doubled, 3m doubled, 2NT doubled all get the game bonus if made
  • The sillier the opponents' double, the more points you get. 2♠ doubled NV making 4 is worth 670 (2×2×30 for tricks + 300 "into" NV game bonus + 100×2 doubled overtricks + 50 insult), but 4♠ doubled making 4 is only 590 (4×2×30 + 300 game bonus + 50 insult) — once doubled into game, there's no reason to bid on unless you think slam is there
  • The "into game" bonus applies whenever the regular trick score reaches 100+ once doubled — e.g. 2♠ doubled (→4♠=game), 3♣ doubled (→6♣=game), or 2NT doubled
  • Doubling into slam is not rewarded with a slam bonus
Examples:
1♠ doubled making 1 = 30×2 + 50 insult + 50 partscore = 160
2♠ doubled making 3 (Vul) = 60×2 + 200 doubled overtrick + 500 Vul game bonus + 50 insult = 870
5♦ doubled making 5 (NV) = 100×2 + 50 insult + 300 game = 550

Scoring Redoubled Contracts

Same idea as doubled, at double the rate. If the redouble is "into game," the game bonus (300 NV / 500 V) is still given — e.g. 1♠ redoubled (→4♠=game), 2♣ redoubled (→8♣=game), or 1NT redoubled. Redoubling into slam is not rewarded with a slam bonus.

Examples:
1♣ redoubled making 2 (NV) = 20×4 + 100 insult + 50 partscore + 200 overtrick = 430
2♠ redoubled making 3 (Vul) = 60×4 + 400 doubled overtrick + 500 game bonus + 100 insult = 1240
5♦ redoubled making 5 (NV) = 100×4 + 100 insult + 300 game = 800
